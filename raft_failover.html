Speaker notes: Raft is designed to maintain availability as long as a majority of voting nodes can communicate. In a three-node cluster, two nodes must remain available. If the active node crashes, the remaining nodes can elect a replacement. But Raft does not provide unlimited fault tolerance. If two nodes fail in a three-node cluster, the remaining node cannot form a majority. This is intentional: Raft prioritizes consistency over accepting potentially conflicting writes.
Speaker notes: "Now that we've seen how Raft handles a leader failure, let's look at what happens when that failed server comes back online. In our example, A was the leader and crashed, and B was elected the new leader. Once A is repaired, we restart the Vault service and unseal it. A reconnects to the cluster and synchronizes any log entries it missed while offline. Importantly, A doesn't automatically become the leader again: B remains leader and A returns as a follower. We can verify with vault operator raft list-peers. This is another benefit of Raft: failed servers can recover and catch up without disrupting the rest of the cluster. This assumes a normal restart with intact persistent storage, not catastrophic loss of Raft data or quorum."
